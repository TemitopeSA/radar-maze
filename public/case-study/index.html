Know when your product bets stop being true.

Assumption Radar is a concept for a new layer inside Maze. It keeps a living record of the beliefs a roadmap rests on, watches the research for signs they're weakening, and turns any doubt into a ready-to-launch study in one click.

The problem

Roadmaps rest on assumptions that are set once and never revisited. Teams find out they've drifted when the metrics drop.

The idea

A living register of assumptions, scored by Maze's own evidence, that flags drift and launches a re-test in one click.

Why Maze

It makes the Baseline and Monitor stages of the system of learning real, using parts Maze has already built.

The ask

A 30-minute walkthrough with product leadership, then a 6-week pilot with 5 design partners.

The problem

Good decisions go stale quietly.

Every roadmap is built on beliefs about customers, markets and products. They're true when they're made. Then conditions shift, and nobody re-checks them until the results show up.

Jo's dead-reckoning analogy, drawn out. The original decision wasn't wrong. It just stopped matching the world, and nothing checked.

Stack Overflow assumed developers needed a community to get answers. Shutterstock assumed original images would stay expensive to make. Both were right for years, and the dashboards looked fine right up until they didn't.

Most companies have the same blind spot on a smaller scale. Assumptions live in PRDs, strategy decks and people's heads. Each one has an expiry date, but nobody writes it down.

Maze is unusually well placed to fix this. Its customers already run the studies that confirm or undermine these beliefs. What's missing is a place to connect each belief to its evidence and notice when that evidence turns.

The demand is there. Research is wanted earlier and more often, yet most teams still bring it in after the problem is defined.

The gap

The system of learning has five stages. Maze's product covers two.

Maze's 2026 narrative describes a loop of Baseline, Monitor, Surface, Research and Decide. The product is excellent at Research and has a strong start on Decide. The first three stages have no product behind them yet.

Stage names and order are from Jonathan Widawski's June 2026 essay, “The system of learning: The last competitive advantage.” The coverage assessment is my own reading of the current product.

The product

One drifting belief, from first signal to updated decision.

The prototype follows Dana Mercer, Head of Product at Lumen, a fictional payments app for small businesses. Her team has priced to win on cost. Here's what happens when Maze notices that customers have moved on.

Step 1 Baseline

Every belief gets an owner, a score and an expiry date

Assumptions get their own home next to Projects. Each one shows who owns it, how confident the evidence makes us, when it was last checked and how much research backs it.

  • Statuses are visible at a glance: Holding, Drifting, Expired, Untested.
  • The list sorts by what needs attention, so the work starts at the top.
  • Confidence comes from linked Maze studies, not opinion.
Assumptions · All assumptions
Step 2 Monitor

Maze notices the drift and explains why

Confidence in “customers pick us because we're cheapest” fell from 78 to 41 over eight weeks as new studies came in. Maze flagged it on September 14, when it crossed the drift threshold.

  • The flag shows its working: price mentions fell from 64% to 22% across 212 participants, while payout speed rose from 14% to 41%.
  • It names its sources and limits, and rates its own confidence.
  • A human confirms with “Looks right” or pushes back.

Assumption detail · Overview tab

Step 3 Surface

The evidence speaks in customers' own words

Recent quotes sit next to older ones, so the shift is easy to hear. Earlier feedback is about fees. Recent feedback is about getting paid fast and on time.

  • Every quote links to its participant, study and timestamp.
  • Interview clips are one click away.
  • This is the traceability researchers need before they trust an AI flag.

Assumption detail · Evidence tab

Step 4 Research

One click turns doubt into a rigorous study

“Re-test with Maze” opens the AI Study Builder already drafted from the assumption and its evidence. It recommends AI-moderated interviews plus a short survey.

  • Bias check catches a leading question and suggests a neutral rewrite.
  • Recruitment defaults to the Maze panel: 40 SMB owners in the US.
  • Fresh Eyes is on, excluding 312 people who recently took a Lumen study.

Step 5 Decide

The belief is updated and the decisions that depended on it are flagged

Results come back: instant payouts lead at 46%, trust follows at 27% and price drops to 18%. Maze proposes a revised assumption, and Dana accepts it.

  • The old statement is kept as version 1. The new one starts at 82 confidence.
  • The Q2 pricing page decision (“lead with lowest fees”) moves from At risk to Needs review, and its owner is notified.

The business case

Every flag starts a study. Every study strengthens the record.

Radar creates a usage loop inside existing accounts. It brings new people into Maze, gives teams a recurring reason to run research, and builds a record that gets more valuable over time.

Expansion

New people inside existing accounts

CPOs, strategy leads and product managers who rarely build studies would still watch the assumptions they own. That creates a natural path to more seats.

Usage

A recurring reason to research

Research today often happens when someone remembers to ask. Expiry dates and drift flags give teams a built-in prompt, which means steady study volume and panel usage.

Retention

A record that's hard to replace

Beliefs, evidence and decisions linked over years become company memory. No other research tool holds that record, so leaving Maze would mean losing it.

Proposed next steps

Prove it with five customers in six weeks.

The fastest way to test the idea is a small pilot. Partners use a manual version of the register, and the team learns whether they keep coming back to it.
